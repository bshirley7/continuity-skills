# Marketing

## Generalized principles

- Establish relevance by naming the audience or situation and the concrete outcome before introducing supporting detail.
- Show the product mechanism, process, or decision aid near the value claim so differentiation is inspectable rather than merely asserted.
- Sequence evidence according to the claim and decision risk, using adoption proof, quantitative support, process detail, or safeguards where each is relevant.
- Use one primary action aligned with the promised outcome, and include a secondary action only when it represents a meaningful alternative readiness level or role.
- Keep the claim, its evidence, and the resulting action continuous enough that users do not have to reconstruct why they should proceed.
- Make qualifications and limits legible at the same scan level as any quantified, regulated, or high-consequence promise.

## Variation levers

- Lead with an audience label when the offer is role-specific, or with a recognizable situation when several audiences share the same outcome.
- Use a product interface, process steps, comparison aid, or contextual artifact according to what makes the differentiated mechanism easiest to verify.
- Place adoption proof near broad credibility claims and place safeguards, qualifications, or process evidence near high-risk or regulated claims.
- Pair a direct commitment action with a lower-commitment inspection path only when the two choices have distinct and understandable purposes.
- Use a single starting action when the section targets one intent; separate distinct intents spatially when several entry models are necessary.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- Audience specificity increases relevance while narrowing perceived applicability.
- Visible product evidence improves comprehension while reducing space for emotional or brand-led storytelling.
- More proof can reduce uncertainty while overwhelming the primary claim and action.
- Dual actions serve different readiness levels while increasing choice and hierarchy demands.
- Quantified claims sharpen value while requiring prominent qualifications that can slow scanning.
- Aspirational imagery can create desire while leaving the product mechanism or service process unclear.

## Failure modes

- A generic action appears before the section establishes audience relevance or a concrete outcome.
- The claim is supported only by decorative imagery rather than product, process, comparison, or evidence.
- Customer logos, ratings, or statistics appear without a clear relationship to the stated promise.
- Several calls to action use similar emphasis despite representing different commitment levels.
- The action label does not continue the task or outcome promised by the section.
- Qualifications are technically present but too small, distant, or visually weak to inform the decision.
- A product preview is too ornamental or illegible to demonstrate the claimed mechanism.

## Anti-patterns

- Leading with an unqualified superlative that could describe any competing product.
- Using a social-proof wall before explaining who the offer serves or why it matters.
- Presenting several equally weighted trial, demo, purchase, contact, and exploration actions in one decision region.
- Using personalization or automation language without showing the input, mechanism, result, or control it provides.
- Separating material limitations from a quantified or high-consequence promise through low-contrast fine print.
- Treating emotional lifestyle imagery as sufficient evidence for a complex service.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Can the intended audience recognize its situation and desired outcome before reading supporting detail?
- Can a user see how the product or service produces the differentiated value?
- Does each proof element support the claim beside it rather than adding unrelated credibility?
- Is one action visibly primary, and does every secondary action represent a distinct readiness level or role?
- Does the action continue the exact task or outcome the section promises?
- Are material qualifications and limits legible where the user evaluates the claim?
- Would the section remain meaningful if its brand name and decorative imagery were removed?
