# Behavioral ethics

## Generalized principles

- Before designing a behavioral intervention, state the user outcome, behavioral mechanism, organizational incentive, expected benefit, plausible harm, affected groups, counter-hypothesis, and stop condition; an uplift target alone is not an ethical rationale.
- Evaluate the full journey and repeated exposure, including defaults, order, visual prominence, friction, urgency, personalization, data feedback loops, and the symmetry of accepting, refusing, correcting, withdrawing, and leaving; a locally clear screen can still produce cumulative coercion.
- Measure user welfare alongside business outcomes through comprehension, voluntary refusal, correction, delayed regret, complaint, trust, task value, distributional effects, and durable control; conversion, engagement, completion, or consent rate cannot establish benefit by themselves.
- Require heightened review, data minimization, safer defaults, representative research, and an accessible non-personalized path when decisions affect children, vulnerable people, privacy, health, finance, essential services, or when targeting can exploit inferred susceptibility.

## Method

- Write an intervention brief covering the user goal, mechanism, organizational incentive, evidence, affected population, potential harms, counter-hypothesis, safeguards, owner, and stop condition.
- Map the complete journey, repeated prompts, defaults, prominence, friction, timing, personalization inputs, refusal and withdrawal paths, recovery, and downstream data use.
- Review the intervention through autonomy, welfare, fairness, transparency, accessibility, privacy, and cumulative-exposure lenses before choosing success measures.
- Test comprehension and voluntary choice before optimization, including refusal, correction, interruption, delayed reflection, and the experience of groups who may carry disproportionate harm.
- Monitor both aggregate and segmented outcomes, complaints, reversals, regret signals, support demand, and unintended behavior; pause when safeguards or stop conditions are crossed.
- Retire or revise interventions whose benefit depends on confusion, pressure, hidden information, asymmetric effort, exploitative targeting, or harm displaced onto less visible groups.

## Ethical safeguards

- Do not treat legal compliance as sufficient evidence of user benefit or freely made choice.
- Do not optimize an intervention until comprehension, refusal, correction, and withdrawal work.
- Do not personalize pressure using sensitive traits, inferred vulnerability, distress, scarcity, or dependency.
- Do not conceal the organizational incentive or a material conflict with the user's goal.
- Do not average away harms concentrated in a smaller or less powerful group.
- Give high-consequence interventions an accountable owner, review record, monitoring window, and enforceable stop condition.

## Variation levers

- Increase review depth with consequence, vulnerability, irreversibility, data sensitivity, exposure frequency, and personalization power.
- Use neutral explanation for ordinary low-risk choices and deliberative friction for consequential commitments, while preserving a clear exit.
- Prefer contextual assistance over behavioral pressure when uncertainty or inability, rather than motivation, is the primary barrier.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- More explicit choice supports autonomy while adding cognitive burden.
- Personalization can increase relevance while creating opaque influence and unequal treatment.
- Short-term completion can conflict with informed preference, delayed welfare, and durable trust.
- Protective friction can prevent harm while becoming obstruction when applied selectively or without a clear rationale.

## Failure modes

- The intervention brief describes the company metric but no user benefit or harm hypothesis.
- A single screen passes review while repeated prompts, defaults, and withdrawal friction create cumulative pressure.
- Aggregate uplift hides lower comprehension, higher regret, or disproportionate harm for a subgroup.
- Consent or engagement is counted as success even when refusal, correction, or later withdrawal is difficult.
- An experiment continues after a safeguard threshold is crossed because the business metric improved.

## Anti-patterns

- Conversion as welfare.
- Compliance as ethics.
- One-screen review.
- Consent-rate optimization.
- Personalized pressure.
- Average away the harm.
- Stop condition without an owner.

## Acceptance and review questions

- What user outcome and behavioral mechanism does the intervention claim, and whose incentive does it serve?
- What harms, affected groups, counter-hypothesis, safeguards, owner, and stop condition are recorded?
- Does the full journey preserve comparable ability to refuse, correct, withdraw, and leave?
- What evidence shows comprehension and user welfare beyond conversion or engagement?
- Which groups may experience disproportionate pressure, exclusion, loss, or data exposure?
- How are repeated exposure, personalization, delayed regret, complaints, reversals, and segmented outcomes monitored?
