# Authorship, provenance, and content integrity

## Generalized principles

- Model provenance through versioned artifacts, activities and accountable contributors: record what was used, generated, transformed, selected, edited, approved and published; identify source and output versions, relevant roles, times and relationships; preserve interoperable lineage across export and recomposition; and distinguish evidence that a process or assertion is intact from judgments about truth, quality, legality, consent or ownership.
- Separate contribution, authorship, ownership, accountability and disclosure: describe human creative contribution and organizational responsibility without crediting an automated tool as a moral or legal actor; avoid inferring rights from possession, prompting or metadata alone; record material assistance and source use; and make disclosure proportional to audience, context, deception risk, consequence and applicable obligation.
- Preserve source, rights and transformation lineage without exposing people unnecessarily: bind ingredients, prompts or instructions where appropriate, tools and versions, edits, crops, composites, translations, transcodes and generated regions to the artifact; preserve consent and license scope separately; support protected or redacted provenance when disclosure creates safety or privacy risk; and do not make invisible metadata the only path to material information.
- Verify content integrity through independent checks: test factual claims against cited evidence, preserve quote and attribution boundaries, review privacy, consent, likeness and voice authority, copyright and license scope, accessibility and foreseeable harm; treat credentials, watermarks, labels and detectors as fallible signals; never imply that credentialed content is true or that missing credentials prove fabrication.
- Make correction, contestation and retraction part of provenance: preserve immutable version identity and the reasons, actor, time and scope of updates; issue linked corrections or retractions instead of silent replacement; propagate changed status to known destinations where feasible; give affected people a review and redress path; and retain only the evidence needed to understand what changed without perpetuating harmful content unnecessarily.

## Method

- Inventory every source artifact, generated or transformed contribution, human edit, version, contributor role, approval and publication destination.
- Map provenance as relationships among artifacts, activities and accountable people or organizations rather than as one creator label.
- Review factual support, quotation, attribution, privacy, consent, likeness or voice authority, rights, licenses, accessibility and foreseeable harm independently of provenance validity.
- Choose visible and machine-readable disclosure based on materiality, audience, deception risk, consequence and legal or policy obligation.
- Test credential and detector interfaces for false confidence, absent metadata, removed metadata, redaction, adversarial manipulation and accessibility.
- Design updates, corrections, retractions, syndication notices and affected-person redress before initial publication.

## Ethical safeguards

- Do not use provenance to launder copied, unlicensed, non-consensual or deceptively transformed source material.
- Do not claim human authorship, endorsement, consent, ownership or institutional approval that the evidence does not support.
- Do not enable deceptive impersonation, synthetic likeness or voice use without authority, contextual disclosure and safeguards proportionate to harm.
- Do not present a credential, watermark, label or detector score as proof of truth, legality, quality or harmlessness.
- Do not remove or obscure material provenance merely to avoid disclosure; preserve a visible explanation when metadata cannot travel.
- Do not expose confidential prompts, protected sources, personal data or vulnerable creators through excessive provenance detail.
- Do not stigmatize assistive technology, translation, accessibility support or routine low-materiality tooling with misleading labels.
- Do not allow rights uncertainty, missing consent or unresolved attribution to disappear behind a generic warning.
- Do not silently revise consequential public content; preserve correction and retraction evidence with a redress path.

## Variation levers

- Use passage- or region-level provenance for consequential claims, quotations and composite media; use artifact-level summaries when assistance is routine and low risk.
- Increase visible disclosure and independent review with public reach, impersonation potential, political or public-interest context, vulnerability and irreversibility.
- Use protected provenance or role-based access where full source disclosure would endanger people, violate confidentiality or expose private creative process.
- Pair machine-readable credentials with accessible human explanation and preserve both through export, syndication and correction.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- Detailed provenance improves accountability while threatening privacy, source protection and creative confidentiality.
- Strong disclosure reduces deception while overbroad labels can imply a false binary between pure human and pure machine work.
- Cryptographic integrity strengthens tamper evidence while encouraging false confidence about the underlying claim.
- Durable history supports correction while retaining harmful or personal content can prolong injury.
- Interoperable metadata aids downstream use while platforms and transformations can remove or invalidate it.

## Failure modes

- A creator field collapses source, prompt writer, editor, rights holder, approver and publisher into one identity.
- Valid provenance is presented as proof that the content is true, licensed, consensual or safe.
- Missing or stripped credentials are presented as proof that content is synthetic or deceptive.
- A disclosure says AI was used but does not identify the material contribution, affected region or accountable publisher.
- Source and transformation history is lost during export, cropping, compositing, translation or syndication.
- Prompts or source metadata expose confidential information or vulnerable contributors without necessity.
- Human edits erase generated lineage or generated transformations erase source attribution.
- Rights, consent and likeness authority are inferred from upload, possession or tool access.
- A correction silently overwrites the published artifact and leaves distributed copies unaddressed.

## Anti-patterns

- One creator field.
- Credential means true.
- No credential means fake.
- Generic assistance label.
- Lineage lost on export.
- Provenance as surveillance.
- Human edit erases history.
- Upload means rights.
- Silent correction.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Are source and output artifacts, activities, versions, contributor roles, transformations, approvals and publication destinations represented without collapsing them into one creator label?
- Does the design distinguish contribution, authorship, ownership, accountability and disclosure without treating an automated tool as the responsible actor?
- Are source, prompt or instruction, tool, transformation, rights, consent and license relationships preserved at a useful granularity without unnecessary exposure?
- Are factual support, quotation, attribution, privacy, consent, likeness or voice authority, rights, accessibility and harm reviewed independently of provenance validity?
- Can people understand what credentials, labels and detectors do and do not establish, including missing, stripped, redacted or invalid metadata?
- Do corrections and retractions preserve versioned reasons, actors, scope, propagation and redress without silently rewriting history?
