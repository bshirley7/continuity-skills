# Color

## Generalized principles

- Choose palette character—warm or cool, muted or saturated, monochrome or multicolor, light or dark—from brand voice, content, environment, task duration, and industry credibility; do not reduce industries to fixed color stereotypes.
- Assign separate semantic roles for brand, surface, text, border, focus, selection, interaction, status, severity, data, and decorative color, then preserve each role across components and themes without allowing one hue to carry conflicting meanings.
- Build neutral surfaces and text as a complete hierarchy with tested contrast for default, subtle, disabled, read-only, hover, focus, selected, overlay, and inverse contexts; tune dark themes independently rather than mathematically inverting light themes.
- Use semantic color proportionally to consequence and always pair status, severity, gain, loss, risk, success, warning, and error with text, iconography, shape, position, or pattern that preserves meaning without color.
- Design data palettes for the analytical task—categorical distinction, ordered magnitude, or diverging direction—with stable mapping, perceptual separation, direct labels or legends, and non-color encoding for critical comparisons.
- Evaluate colors in realistic combinations, states, themes, displays, ambient conditions, color-vision differences, localization, and variable user content; validate contrast and meaning at the component and workflow level rather than from isolated swatches.

## Variation levers

- Use a restrained neutral system with one brand accent for dense or high-consequence products.
- Use broader chromatic expression in marketing, creation, and exploration while keeping operational states semantically stable.
- Use warm palettes for human or tactile expression and cool palettes for technical restraint only when the choice is authentic rather than stereotyped.
- Use sequential palettes for magnitude, diverging palettes around a meaningful midpoint, and categorical palettes only for discrete groups.
- Offer light, dark, and high-contrast themes through shared semantic roles with independently tuned values.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- A distinctive brand palette improves recognition while competing with semantic color.
- Restrained color improves focus while making boundaries and disabled states harder to perceive.
- Saturation attracts attention while creating fatigue and false urgency.
- Dark themes reduce luminous intensity while compressing low-contrast structure.
- Many data colors support more series while reducing reliable distinction.
- Industry familiarity increases credibility while reinforcing visual sameness and cultural assumptions.

## Failure modes

- One brand hue means action, selection, information, and success.
- A dark theme is an inverted light palette.
- Subtle text, borders, or disabled states disappear.
- Alert colors are used decoratively and lose urgency.
- Status or gain-loss meaning depends on red and green alone.
- A categorical chart has more series than users can reliably distinguish.
- Colors pass isolated contrast checks but fail over real surfaces or user content.
- An industry palette is chosen from stereotype rather than brand and task fit.

## Anti-patterns

- Finance equals blue.
- Health equals green.
- Luxury equals black and gold.
- Technology equals neon gradient.
- Red and green only.
- Brand color for every button.
- Opacity as the only disabled treatment.
- Rainbow categorical chart.
- Automatic dark-mode inversion.
- Contrast checked on swatches only.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Does palette character follow brand, content, environment, duration, and credibility rather than industry stereotype?
- Are brand, surface, text, border, focus, selection, interaction, status, severity, data, and decorative roles distinct?
- Do neutral hierarchies preserve every state and are light, dark, and high-contrast values tuned independently?
- Are status, severity, gain, loss, risk, success, warning, and error understandable without color?
- Does each data palette match categorical, sequential, or diverging meaning with stable mapping and non-color support?
- Have realistic combinations, displays, ambient conditions, color-vision differences, localization, and user content been tested?
- Do color tokens remain semantically consistent across components, themes, workflows, and platforms?
