# High-consequence decisions

## Generalized principles

- Place current state, target state, billing or timing consequence, affected scope, and the exact commitment in one reviewable decision frame.
- For destructive actions, name the concrete objects and capabilities that will be lost, whether loss is immediate or delayed, what can be exported or transferred, and whether recovery remains possible.
- Scale confirmation friction to consequence: ordinary plan selection needs clear review, while irreversible deletion may require identity verification or a target-specific phrase without obscuring a visible cancel path.
- After commitment, show a durable resulting state with effective date, retained access, next charge or refund state, recovery window, and the available correction or restart path.

## Variation levers

- Use side-by-side cards for few tiers and a matrix for materially different capabilities.
- Separate recommendations from factual comparisons and explain the basis for any recommendation.
- Use a staged review when multiple people, resources, or legal consequences are affected.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- Retention alternatives may reveal useful options while creating pressure at the exit point.
- Typed confirmation increases deliberation while adding burden and potentially becoming ritual friction.
- Dense comparison tables improve completeness while making material differences harder to identify.

## Failure modes

- The recommendation badge substitutes for explaining fit.
- Annual savings emphasis obscures total commitment or renewal terms.
- A loss inventory uses generic categories that do not match the user's actual holdings.
- Cancellation confirmation is transient and leaves the resulting entitlement state ambiguous.

## Anti-patterns

- Generic OK for irreversible deletion.
- Keep button visually dominant over a valid exit.
- Feature-count theater.
- Consequences disclosed only after commitment.
- Typed confirmation without a target-specific consequence.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Can the user identify current and resulting state without inference?
- Are total cost, timing, scope, and material exclusions visible before commitment?
- Does destructive confirmation name actual affected objects and recovery limits?
- Is refusal or cancellation available with comparable clarity and effort?
- Does the final state verify what happened and what remains possible?
