# Trust and risk reversal

## Generalized principles

- Name the specific uncertainty or risk at the decision point, then show the relevant safeguard, evidence, remaining limitation, accountable party, and next step rather than using generic reassurance.
- Describe reversibility precisely: what can be cancelled, refunded, restored, revoked, archived, downgraded, exported, or deleted; who can do it; through which path; within what time; at what cost; and what state or data remains.
- Place price, renewal, eligibility, scope, exclusions, dependencies, downstream effects, and material policy terms before commitment and keep them adjacent to the action they govern.
- Scale warning, friction, verification, and confirmation to impact, scope, irreversibility, sensitivity, and accidental-action likelihood; offer a safer reversible alternative when one exists.
- For shared objects, permissions, integrations, and automation, identify affected people, connected systems, active or scheduled work, credentials, notifications, audit history, and who can recover or revoke the change.
- Make refusal, delay, cancellation, and limited-access alternatives understandable and proportionate; trust is weakened when the only practical path is acceptance or when declining is framed as user failure.

## Variation levers

- Use inline terms for common low-risk commitments and a review step for variable or high-impact commitments.
- Use archive or disable as the default when permanent deletion is not required.
- Require object-name entry or secondary verification only for broad, irreversible, or security-sensitive actions.
- Show a concise risk summary with inspectable policy, evidence, and audit detail.
- Offer a limited path without optional permissions when the core task can still function.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- More reassurance reduces anxiety while risking false certainty.
- More friction prevents accidents while blocking confident expert work.
- Concise terms improve scanning while hiding exclusions or downstream effects.
- Reversible defaults improve safety while increasing retained data and administration.
- Strong warnings attract attention while becoming ritualized through overuse.
- Permission bundling simplifies setup while weakening informed control.

## Failure modes

- A trust badge appears without naming the control or evidence it represents.
- Cancel anytime omits the path, timing, cost, or retained access.
- A refund promise hides exclusions or consumed-content rules.
- Delete is used for archive, detach, revoke, and permanent destruction interchangeably.
- A confirmation asks whether the user is sure without describing consequences.
- Declining permission blocks unrelated core functionality.
- A reversible action is presented as permanent or an irreversible action as recoverable.

## Anti-patterns

- Trust us.
- Risk-free without limits.
- Cancel anytime without process.
- Guaranteed without eligibility.
- Are you sure.
- Red dialog for every deletion.
- Bundled permission consent.
- Decline means exit.
- Archive labeled delete.
- Undo promise without retention window.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Is the specific risk named with safeguard, evidence, limitation, owner, and next step?
- Does reversibility state what, who, path, time, cost, and remaining state?
- Are price, renewal, eligibility, scope, exclusions, dependencies, and downstream effects adjacent to commitment?
- Does friction scale with impact, scope, irreversibility, sensitivity, and accident likelihood?
- Are affected people, systems, work, credentials, notifications, history, and recovery ownership visible?
- Can users refuse, delay, cancel, or choose limited access without disproportionate penalty?
- Do reassurance claims avoid implying certainty beyond the demonstrated safeguard or policy?
