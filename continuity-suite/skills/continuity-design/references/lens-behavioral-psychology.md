# Behavioral psychology

## Generalized principles

- Treat every behavioral principle as a context-bound hypothesis, not a universal law: name the user, task, environment, cognitive constraint, predicted effect, possible counter-effect, affected groups, and evidence needed to accept or reject it.
- Evaluate the decision cycle across attention, meaning, action, and memory: make important information perceivable, support an accurate mental model, keep effort and consequences proportionate, and preserve recognition, continuity, and recovery.
- Reduce avoidable cognitive burden through clear hierarchy, familiar controls, plain language, meaningful grouping, recognition over recall, visible system state, and information available at the step where it is needed.
- Use defaults, framing, social information, scarcity, progress, rewards, urgency, and loss-related messaging only when they clarify real conditions and support the user's stated goal; never use them to conceal alternatives, manufacture pressure, or make refusal harder.
- Preserve autonomy with a visible neutral alternative, informed consequence, proportional friction, granular consent, correction, exit, undo, and recovery; make the easiest path compatible with the user's interests rather than only the organization's preferred outcome.
- Validate behavioral hypotheses with representative and inclusive research plus task evidence; separate observed behavior, participant explanation, analytics, accessibility findings, expert interpretation, and business outcomes, and monitor delayed or second-order effects.

## Method

- Define the user decision, desired user outcome, consequence, environment, time pressure, prior knowledge, and accessibility context.
- Diagnose the dominant constraint in attention, meaning, action, or memory using observed evidence rather than stakeholder assumption.
- Select no more than three relevant behavioral principles and state why each applies.
- Write a behavioral hypothesis, counter-hypothesis, expected observable effect, affected groups, and stopping condition.
- Design the intervention together with autonomy, accessibility, transparency, reversal, and recovery safeguards.
- Test comprehension, task success, errors, time, confidence, accessibility, trust, refusal, recovery, and downstream behavior.
- Record evidence, limitations, contradictions, unintended consequences, and whether the principle should be retained, revised, or rejected.

## Ethical safeguards

- Do not optimize a proxy behavior when it conflicts with the user's stated outcome or welfare.
- Do not manufacture scarcity, urgency, social proof, authority, progress, or loss.
- Do not hide material terms, alternatives, costs, risk, cancellation, privacy, or consequences behind progressive disclosure.
- Do not exploit fatigue, fear, confusion, cognitive impairment, inexperience, or time pressure.
- Require a neutral path and comparable effort for refusal, correction, cancellation, and recovery.
- Include disabled users and people under realistic stress, interruption, and device conditions in evaluation.

## Variation levers

- Use a lightweight hypothesis card for low-consequence familiar interactions and a formal review for financial, health, privacy, consent, or irreversible decisions.
- Apply behavioral principles to comprehension and task support before applying them to conversion or retention.
- Use progressive disclosure for optional complexity while keeping material decision information visible.
- Use reminders and progress cues that users can configure, pause, dismiss, or disable.
- Prefer durable learning and recognition over repeated prompts or attention capture.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- Reducing choices lowers immediate burden while removing meaningful control.
- Familiar patterns improve recognition while preserving flawed conventions.
- Defaults accelerate completion while silently steering consequential decisions.
- Progress cues support motivation while creating pressure or false completion.
- Personalization increases relevance while narrowing exposure and obscuring inference.
- Memorable peaks improve recall while distracting from end-to-end task quality.

## Failure modes

- A principle is applied because its name sounds relevant rather than because evidence supports the mechanism.
- A behavioral effect is treated as universal across users, cultures, abilities, and contexts.
- A conversion metric is used as proof of comprehension, satisfaction, or welfare.
- Material information is hidden to reduce cognitive load.
- The easiest path serves the organization while refusal requires more effort.
- Short-term engagement improves while trust, learning, or long-term outcomes worsen.
- Accessibility and cognitive disability are considered only after the intervention is selected.
- Contradictory evidence and harmed segments disappear inside averages.

## Anti-patterns

- Bias bingo.
- Psychology says users will.
- One metric proves the design.
- Ethical because it is subtle.
- Default without a neutral alternative.
- Urgency without a real deadline.
- Social proof without provenance.
- Progress without meaningful completion.
- Delight as compensation for friction.
- Dark patterns renamed as nudges.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Is the behavioral principle framed as a falsifiable, context-bound hypothesis?
- Which attention, meaning, action, or memory constraint is supported by evidence?
- Does the intervention preserve accurate understanding, autonomy, accessibility, refusal, correction, exit, and recovery?
- Are defaults, framing, social signals, urgency, scarcity, progress, rewards, and loss claims truthful and proportionate?
- Are material terms and consequences visible before the decision they govern?
- Does evaluation include representative users, disabled users, realistic pressure, counter-effects, harmed segments, and delayed outcomes?
- Are observed behavior, explanation, analytics, accessibility evidence, interpretation, and business outcomes kept distinct?
