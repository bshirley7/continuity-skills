# Proof first

## Generalized principles

- Lead with proof when the claim is consequential, unfamiliar, comparative, broad, or easy to overstate, but first provide enough audience and problem context for the reader to understand what the evidence is meant to verify.
- Name the evidence type and keep it distinct: a product state demonstrates mechanism, a customer result demonstrates an observed outcome, a benchmark demonstrates comparison, an assurance demonstrates control, and a testimonial demonstrates attributed experience.
- Make quantitative proof self-describing by attaching the measured outcome to the actor or population, intervention or product use, timeframe, baseline or comparator, unit, and source needed to judge the number.
- Use product demonstrations to expose the relevant input, mechanism, state change, and output, and identify whether the view is representative, illustrative, live, or a selected example.
- Bind customer evidence to a named context, role or organization, concrete outcome, implementation footprint, and an inspection path; use logos only as breadth context, never as outcome proof.
- Explain external assurance through the standard or issuer, present status, scope, applicability, and a route to current detail instead of treating a badge as a complete trust argument.
- For comparative proof, show the tested alternatives, metric definition, direction of better performance, dataset or sample, conditions, and claim boundary before interpreting the result.
- Place evidence close to the claim and decision it governs, then state the interpretation and offer a proportionate next action without requiring personal data before representative proof is visible.
- Layer complementary evidence only when each form answers a different question, and establish a clear order among demonstration, measured result, external validation, testimony, and adoption breadth.
- Preserve uncertainty and transferability limits: identify when evidence comes from one customer, selected examples, commissioned research, a particular dataset, or a specific operating context.

## Variation levers

- Use a product state first when mechanism is the main uncertainty, a measured outcome first when impact is the main uncertainty, and external assurance first when control or risk is the main uncertainty.
- Use one high-quality proof item for a narrow claim, and use a structured set when different audiences or decision risks require distinct evidence types.
- Use a compact metric card when actor, outcome, unit, timeframe, and source fit visibly; otherwise use a concise summary linked to the full case study or report.
- Use a static interface state for rapid inspection, a controlled demonstration for workflow or change over time, and an interactive example only when the user can recover and understand what is representative.
- Use testimony to explain lived experience, measured results to support impact, certifications to support controls, and adoption marks only to show breadth.
- Place methodology inline when it changes interpretation materially, and use a linked report when detail is extensive while preserving key qualifications at the claim.
- Use independent evidence for high-risk or comparative claims when available, and label commissioned, internal, selected, or customer-reported evidence explicitly.
- Lead with brief relevance context before proof when the audience cannot otherwise tell what is being measured or why it matters.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- Leading with evidence increases credibility while delaying the emotional or conceptual framing that makes the evidence relevant.
- Compact metrics support scanning while omitting baseline, timeframe, sample, method, or transferability limits.
- Product imagery makes a mechanism tangible while presenting an idealized or selected state as representative behavior.
- Customer stories create concrete context while overgeneralizing from one implementation to a broad product claim.
- Independent studies increase authority while commissioned scope, dated conditions, and selected reporting still require disclosure.
- Security badges compress complex assurance while obscuring status, scope, applicability, and expiration.
- Multiple evidence forms create breadth while encouraging badge walls, logo walls, and unprioritized proof density.
- Methodology improves inspectability while overwhelming users who only need a bounded decision summary.

## Failure modes

- The user must submit contact information before seeing representative product, outcome, benchmark, or assurance evidence.
- A screenshot or animation is presented as proof even though it does not expose input, mechanism, state change, or output.
- A number omits the actor or population, outcome definition, unit, timeframe, baseline, comparator, or source.
- A selected customer outcome is generalized into a typical or universal product result without qualification.
- A testimonial, logo, award, certification, benchmark, and adoption count are treated as interchangeable evidence.
- A trust mark appears without current status, scope, applicability, issuer context, or a route to detail.
- A benchmark chart omits tested alternatives, metric direction, dataset, conditions, or the boundary of the conclusion.
- Proof is placed far from the claim or action it governs, leaving the reader to infer the relationship.
- Several proof types compete visually without a hierarchy tied to the user's actual uncertainty.
- The interpretation is stronger than the demonstrated state, customer case, study scope, dataset, or measurement supports.

## Anti-patterns

- Calling a gated sales form a demonstration while showing no representative evidence before submission.
- Using a polished interface collage as proof of an end-to-end workflow or completed outcome.
- Displaying large percentages, multipliers, or currency figures without a visible denominator, period, baseline, and source.
- Presenting customer marks as evidence that named outcomes occurred.
- Quoting an enthusiastic customer without role, organization, implementation context, or observable result.
- Stacking security, review, and award badges into one undifferentiated trust wall.
- Publishing a comparison chart whose preferred result depends on an unexplained metric or selected test set.
- Hiding methodology and material qualifications in a distant report while repeating the headline claim near conversion.
- Using one exceptional customer or selected example to imply default product performance.
- Treating more proof artifacts as inherently stronger than a smaller, coherent, decision-relevant evidence chain.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Does the reader understand the audience, problem, and claim that the leading evidence is meant to verify?
- Is each artifact correctly identified as demonstration, customer result, benchmark, assurance, testimony, or adoption breadth?
- Do quantitative results expose actor or population, intervention, outcome, unit, timeframe, baseline or comparator, and source?
- Does a product demonstration show the relevant input, mechanism, state change, and output, with representative status identified?
- Are customer outcomes tied to named context, implementation footprint, and a route to the full case rather than inferred from logos?
- Do external assurances expose standard or issuer, status, scope, applicability, and a current detail path?
- Do comparisons expose alternatives, metric, direction, dataset or sample, conditions, and claim boundary?
- Is proof adjacent to the claim and action it governs, with representative evidence visible before personal-data requests?
- Does each additional proof type answer a distinct decision question within a clear evidence hierarchy?
- Are selected cases, commissioned studies, dataset limits, and contextual transferability stated before the interpretation becomes broader than the evidence?
