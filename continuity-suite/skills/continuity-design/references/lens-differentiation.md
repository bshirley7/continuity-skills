# Differentiation

## Generalized principles

- Differentiate first through the product's domain model, workflow sequence, evidence relationships, defaults, and distinctive user capability; preserve learned conventions for navigation, selection, actions, state, history, and recovery unless changing them creates a demonstrated benefit.
- Build recognition through a coherent visual and content grammar—typography, composition, illustration, motion, density, terminology, and information treatment—while keeping operational labels and interaction semantics direct.
- Tie expressive choices to the product mechanism, audience, or artifacts users create; pair metaphor and category cues with concrete examples, evidence, constraints, and a clear action.
- Concentrate novelty in a few memorable moments and let frequent, dense, consequential, and assistive workflows use stable patterns so recognition does not create repeated learning cost.
- In financial, security, health, permission, and irreversible decisions, preserve conventional review, evidence, warning, confirmation, and recovery structure even when surrounding expression is distinctive.
- Evaluate differentiation against comprehension, task success, accessibility, performance, localization, trust, maintenance, and recognizable value—not aesthetic novelty or competitor distance alone.

## Variation levers

- Use a distinctive domain object or workflow when it directly improves the user's work.
- Place the strongest expression in onboarding, empty states, creation moments, transitions, and marketing rather than every control.
- Use one or two signature visual behaviors consistently instead of many unrelated effects.
- Adapt density and expression by consequence: expressive exploration, restrained commitment.
- Combine category familiarity with a product-specific mechanism, proof pattern, or point of view.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- Familiar patterns reduce learning while increasing category sameness.
- Distinctive interaction can reveal a better model while imposing migration and accessibility cost.
- Strong expression improves recognition while competing with content and performance.
- Category cues build immediate understanding while encouraging visual convergence.
- A consistent brand grammar improves cohesion while becoming monotonous in dense work.
- Competitor contrast sharpens positioning while encouraging reactive design.

## Failure modes

- Standard controls are renamed to sound proprietary.
- A new interaction model has no user benefit beyond novelty.
- Brand expression obscures the product mechanism, evidence, or action.
- Every surface carries the same high level of visual intensity.
- A metaphor replaces concrete behavior and constraints.
- Consequential workflows sacrifice familiar review or confirmation for style.
- Differentiation depends on motion, color, imagery, or performance-heavy effects alone.

## Anti-patterns

- Novelty as strategy.
- Proprietary name for a standard button.
- Signature interaction everywhere.
- Competitor screenshot inversion.
- Category aesthetic without product mechanism.
- Metaphor instead of evidence.
- Branded error that hides recovery.
- Expressive checkout.
- Animation-only identity.
- Accessibility treated as visual compromise.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Does differentiation arise from the domain model, workflow, evidence, defaults, or a meaningful user capability?
- Which learned conventions remain stable, and what demonstrated benefit justifies each changed convention?
- Is the visual and content grammar coherent while operational labels and semantics remain direct?
- Are expressive choices tied to product mechanism, audience, artifacts, examples, evidence, and constraints?
- Is novelty concentrated away from frequent, dense, consequential, and assistive work?
- Do high-consequence flows preserve conventional review, warning, confirmation, and recovery?
- Has differentiation been evaluated for comprehension, success, accessibility, performance, localization, trust, maintenance, and recognizable value?
