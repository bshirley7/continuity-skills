# Symbol comprehension and redundant coding

## Generalized principles

- Treat every symbol as a semantic contract, not a picture: define its concept, role, action or destination, object and state, plain-language name, accessible exposure, allowable variants and prohibited synonyms; reuse it consistently only where those semantics remain true; and separate interface icons, pictograms, illustrations, logos, emoji and data marks because they carry different interaction and interpretation obligations.
- Use a symbol alone only when recognition is strongly established in the specific audience and context and error cost is low: otherwise add a persistent visible label, direct state text, tooltip or instruction; describe an interactive symbol by what it does or where it goes rather than its shape; keep the accessible name consistent with visible words; and never require hover, vision or prior product familiarity to discover essential meaning.
- Make meaning redundantly perceivable without producing duplicate noise: combine the semantic label with appropriate shape, container, position, contrast, pattern, text or programmatic state; hide decorative and fully text-redundant imagery from assistive output; expose nonvisual alternatives for meaningful symbols; and ensure color, outline or fill, motion, orientation and spatial position are never the sole carrier of selection, severity, availability or completion.
- Design the visual form for perception at the point of use: use a bounded grid while adjusting optical size, alignment, weight and negative space; simplify details at small sizes; preserve non-text contrast; match adjacent typography and emphasis; place the visible glyph inside an adequately sized target; separate neighboring targets; scale meaningful symbols with user settings; and test across themes, density, zoom, low vision and motor variability.
- Represent states and consequence as claims that can be checked: distinguish current destination, active mode, pressed control, disabled action, pending operation, acknowledged request, verified completion, warning, prohibition, stale data and failure with exact language and evidence; do not let a check, lock, shield, cloud, trash, warning or animation overstate security, approval, synchronization or deletion; and scale confirmation and recovery to the harm of misinterpretation.
- Localize symbols by semantics rather than mechanically mirroring assets: review reading direction, movement, chronology, writing, letters, numerals, currency, hand gestures, body and gender, religion, law, transport and domain conventions; mirror directional interfaces only when the underlying meaning reverses; provide script-specific assets and nongendered representations where needed; and retain a plain-language alternative when cultural transfer is uncertain.
- Govern comprehension empirically over the symbol lifecycle: test unaided meaning, action prediction, distinction from adjacent symbols, recognition at actual size, state interpretation, assistive name, keyboard and touch use, localization and error recovery with intended audiences; record semantic ownership, source, version, platform variants and deprecation; audit duplicate and orphan symbols; and treat a vector replacement as a behavior change when meaning or recognition may shift.

## Method

- Inventory every symbol by semantic role, concept, action or destination, object, state, label, accessible name, variant, locale and owner.
- Classify whether the symbol is familiar enough for the intended audience and consequence or requires a persistent label and stronger redundancy.
- Design optical form, target, grouping, interaction and state across actual sizes, themes, density, zoom, keyboard, touch and assistive output.
- Review cultural, directional, textual, human and regulatory meaning for each supported locale and domain.
- Test comprehension and action prediction, then govern semantic tokens, assets, variants, versions, deprecations and regressions.

## Ethical safeguards

- Do not use an unfamiliar or ambiguous symbol as the only route to an essential or consequential action.
- Do not describe a control to assistive technology by visual shape when the needed information is its action or destination.
- Do not make color, fill, motion, direction or position the only state indicator.
- Do not shrink an interactive target merely because the visible symbol is small.
- Do not use a reassuring lock, shield, check or badge as a substitute for actual security, validation or evidence.
- Do not use culturally specific gestures, people, gender, letters, currency or direction as if their meaning were universal.
- Do not expose both redundant icon and visible label to assistive output when that produces duplicated or confusing speech.
- Do not preserve a familiar asset after its action semantics change or replace it without comprehension testing.
- Do not measure icon-system success by visual consistency alone while people cannot predict actions or distinguish states.

## Variation levers

- Require persistent labels as familiarity decreases or consequence, audience diversity and use infrequency increase.
- Increase target size, spacing, contrast and text redundancy with motor, visual, environmental and attention constraints.
- Use platform-native variants when they improve transfer, while preserving canonical semantics and accessible names across platforms.
- Allow denser icon-only expert controls only after task-specific learning, immediate name access, keyboard support and low-cost recovery are demonstrated.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- Compact symbols increase available workspace while increasing recall, visual and motor burden.
- A branded visual language increases distinctiveness while weakening learned platform meaning.
- Redundant labels improve comprehension while reducing density and sometimes duplicating assistive output.
- Uniform geometry creates cohesion while optical and semantic differentiation may require controlled variation.
- Localization improves meaning while increasing asset, testing and governance complexity.

## Failure modes

- Internal teams agree on a glyph but intended users cannot name or predict it.
- The accessible name describes a picture instead of the resulting action.
- Visible label, tooltip, accessible name and actual action use different verbs.
- Selected or warning state disappears in monochrome, reduced motion or high contrast.
- The glyph is legible but its target is too small or crowded to operate.
- A reassuring symbol is shown before the underlying state is verified.
- A directional or textual symbol is mirrored or localized without checking semantic direction.
- Decorative icons are announced repeatedly or meaningful icons are hidden.
- An updated asset silently changes perceived meaning across products.

## Anti-patterns

- Designer recognition as user evidence.
- Picture-name accessibility.
- Four labels for one action.
- Single-channel state.
- Glyph-sized target.
- Premature trust symbol.
- Mechanical mirroring.
- Assistive icon noise.
- Semantic drift by asset update.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Does each symbol have one explicit semantic contract, role, name, accessible exposure, allowed variants and prohibited synonyms?
- Is standalone use justified by demonstrated recognition and low error cost, with visible and assistive labels matching the actual action or destination?
- Is meaning redundant across appropriate text, shape, container, contrast, position and programmatic state without duplicate assistive noise?
- Are optical size, weight, detail, contrast, target, spacing and scaling tested at actual use sizes across themes and access needs?
- Do state and consequence labels distinguish selection, mode, pending, acknowledgement, verification, warning, prohibition, staleness and failure without overclaiming?
- Have direction, script, numbers, currency, gesture, body, gender, religion, law and domain conventions been localized by meaning with plain alternatives?
- Has comprehension, action prediction, distinction, assistive output, operation and recovery been tested and governed across versions and platforms?
