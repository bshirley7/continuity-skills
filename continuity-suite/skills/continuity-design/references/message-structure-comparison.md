# Comparison

## Generalized principles

- Begin with the decision context—who is choosing, the job or outcome, the options in scope, and the few criteria that materially change fit—before presenting a detailed comparison.
- Use stable alignment and consistent language across options; group criteria by buyer concern and visually prioritize meaningful differences while keeping the complete evidence available.
- Represent comparison states precisely—standard, limited, metered, add-on, conditional, unavailable, or unknown—and define technical terms, units, periods, thresholds, and dependencies.
- Explain recommendations and defaults through explicit audience, assumptions, evidence, and tradeoffs; preserve a neutral path to inspect and select every viable option.
- Place total cost, recurring commitment, eligibility, limits, overage behavior, cancellation or switching effects, and material risk before the action they govern.
- Keep comparison usable across narrow screens and assistive technology by preserving option identity, criterion association, reading order, keyboard access, and a non-color expression of every difference.

## Variation levers

- Use concise option cards when differences are few and a grouped matrix when criteria are numerous.
- Lead with audience and outcome for unfamiliar offers; lead with measurable limits for technical or usage-based offers.
- Provide a short decision-driving view with an expandable complete comparison.
- Use a recommendation only when its assumptions and tradeoffs can be stated beside it.
- Offer calculator or configuration support when price depends on quantity, usage, seats, or services.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- Completeness improves diligence while increasing cognitive load.
- A recommendation reduces uncertainty while introducing bias.
- Compact tables improve scanning while degrading on narrow screens.
- Feature parity language simplifies comparison while hiding different limits or quality.
- Progressive disclosure protects focus while risking hidden constraints.
- Outcome framing improves relevance while making unlike options appear directly equivalent.

## Failure modes

- Options use inconsistent labels, units, periods, or criterion order.
- The comparison emphasizes many low-value similarities over decisive differences.
- A checkmark hides a limit, dependency, add-on, or conditional state.
- A recommended option has no stated audience or rationale.
- Total cost or recurring commitment appears after selection.
- Horizontal overflow breaks criterion-to-option association.
- The matrix has no explanation, evidence, or deeper path for ambiguous claims.

## Anti-patterns

- Unexplained best-value badge.
- Checkmark-only comparison.
- Manufactured competitor weakness.
- Different billing periods in the same price row.
- Unlimited without a defined boundary.
- Feature wall with equal emphasis.
- Desktop-only horizontal matrix.
- Preselected option with hidden tradeoffs.
- Contact sales replacing every material term.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Are the decision, audience, options, and material fit criteria clear before the detail?
- Are option labels, units, periods, criterion order, and state language consistent?
- Are included, limited, metered, add-on, conditional, unavailable, and unknown states distinguishable?
- Does every recommendation state its audience, assumptions, evidence, and tradeoff?
- Do total cost, commitment, eligibility, limits, overage, switching effects, and risk precede action?
- Does the comparison preserve identity, association, reading order, keyboard access, and non-color meaning on narrow screens?
- Can readers inspect definitions, evidence, and complete detail without losing their place?
