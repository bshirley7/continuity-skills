# Decision architecture

## Generalized principles

- Present each material choice with its purpose, included and excluded outcomes, risk, cost, timing, affected scope, and correction path before the user commits; never defer a decision-changing fact to a later step.
- Make accept, refuse, defer, revise, and withdraw recognizable and comparably understandable; do not change prominence, effort, defaults, order, or information flow to manufacture the preferred answer.
- Preserve orientation and work across multi-step decisions by showing completed, current, and remaining steps, retaining prior choices, and supporting review, back, correction, and undo without re-entry.
- Treat the expected decision effect and its counter-effect as research questions, then test comprehension, refusal, errors, recovery, affected groups, and delayed outcomes rather than inferring welfare from completion or conversion.

## Method

- Define the user decision, desired user outcome, affected scope, consequence, alternatives, time pressure, and reversibility.
- Inventory every material fact and place it before the choice it can change.
- Compare the visual, semantic, interaction, and effort treatment of accept, refuse, defer, revise, and withdraw.
- Prototype the complete decision and recovery journey, including the resulting state.
- Test unaided comprehension, comparison, refusal, correction, and delayed recall with representative users.
- Record counter-effects and harmed segments separately from aggregate completion.

## Ethical safeguards

- Do not preselect consequential consent or paid additions.
- Do not hide refusal, withdrawal, cancellation, total cost, renewal, data use, or material exclusions.
- Do not use false scarcity, urgency, social proof, or obstruction.
- Do not interpret completion as informed agreement.
- Require comparable access to correction and exit.
- Escalate review for financial, health, privacy, legal, or irreversible decisions.

## Variation levers

- Use a compact comparison for low-consequence familiar choices and a structured review for consequential commitments.
- Use progressive disclosure for optional detail while keeping material facts visible.
- Add confirmation friction only when it helps verify scope, identity, or irreversible intent.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- More visible alternatives improve autonomy while increasing immediate choice complexity.
- Confirmation can prevent errors while becoming ritual or obstructive friction.
- Defaults reduce effort while silently steering decisions.

## Failure modes

- The interface names benefits but not exclusions or downstream effects.
- Refusal exists but requires more steps or less legible controls.
- A summary cannot be corrected without losing work.
- Research measures clicks without testing understanding or welfare.

## Anti-patterns

- Consent by continuation.
- Preselected agreement.
- Reject hidden in settings.
- Material term after commit.
- Undo that requires support.
- Conversion as comprehension.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Are all decision-changing facts visible before commitment?
- Can the user recognize and understand accept, refuse, defer, revise, and withdraw?
- Are alternatives treated with comparable information and effort?
- Can the user review and correct without losing work?
- Does testing measure comprehension, refusal, recovery, and delayed effects?
- Are harmed groups and counter-effects visible rather than averaged away?
