# Editorial

## Generalized principles

- Establish an explicit editorial hierarchy with one lead idea or story, a bounded supporting set, and a separate route into the broader archive; do not make every item compete at the same visual level.
- Treat headline, deck, category or format, author or publisher, date, and reading scope as one orientation system, keeping the metadata close enough to the story that it cannot be mistaken for another item.
- Use image scale, crop, illustration, and contrast to establish editorial tone and priority, while preserving enough adjacent text and metadata for the item to remain understandable without interpreting the image.
- Vary card size or composition only when it communicates a real difference in priority, format, or reading path; preserve a stable content grammar for comparable items.
- Keep topic, format, date, author, and reading-time conventions consistent across a collection so readers can compare items despite expressive variation.
- Separate editorial discovery controls such as search, topic, period, type, sort, and archive state from story-level actions, and keep the active browse state visible.
- Preserve interaction clarity through predictable card boundaries, explicit continuation controls, visible mixed-media cues, and link placement that does not depend on image interpretation or hover alone.
- Use typography to create publication voice through scale, family contrast, measure, and spacing, but retain readable line lengths, sufficient metadata emphasis, and a clear transition into the next content state.
- Give immersive long-form openings ancestry, date, title, and a clear body transition so visual pacing does not erase orientation.
- Offer both immersive and rapid-scanning paths when the audience may alternate between browsing, evaluating relevance, and reading deeply.

## Variation levers

- Use one dominant lead with compact supporting stories when time-sensitive priority matters, or an equal grid when the collection is intentionally peer-ranked.
- Use serif and sans-serif contrast to distinguish editorial voice from utility, or one family with weight and scale changes when brand consistency or dense scanning matters more.
- Use documentary photography for real-world credibility, illustration for explanatory or conceptual stories, and typographic art when the title itself carries the editorial premise.
- Place metadata above the headline for rapid category and recency scanning, below it for a quieter reading introduction, or split it around the image only when association remains unambiguous.
- Use a narrow article measure for long-form reading and a wider modular grid for archives, reports, case studies, and mixed formats.
- Use visible filters when readers arrive with a known topic or period, search for broad archives, and lightweight category links when the collection is small.
- Expose reading time, format, or media cues when commitment differs materially across items; omit them when all items have comparable scope.
- Use restrained motion or carousel behavior only for bounded featured content, with persistent controls and a non-sequential path to the same items.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- A dramatic lead story creates focus while reducing visibility for supporting items and archive utilities.
- Varied card scale creates editorial rhythm while making comparison and responsive reflow more difficult.
- Expressive typography creates a recognizable publication voice while threatening readability, localization, and narrow-screen fit.
- Large imagery creates immersion while increasing load, crop risk, and ambiguity when the text association is weak.
- Rich metadata supports relevance judgments while making cards dense and repetitive.
- Topic filters and archive controls support retrieval while competing with the editorial opening for attention.
- A regular grid supports scanning while flattening meaningful differences in priority, format, or recency.
- Immersive long-form pacing supports depth while delaying orientation, conversion, or a route back to the collection.

## Failure modes

- Every story receives the same visual weight even though one item is recent, foundational, or strategically important.
- A large image or typographic treatment becomes the only clue to a story's meaning, format, or destination.
- Cards vary in size, crop, type, and metadata without a semantic reason, producing decorative rhythm rather than editorial hierarchy.
- Category, author, date, format, and reading time move unpredictably between cards or appear too far from the item they describe.
- A featured carousel hides stories behind sequence, motion, hover, or controls that are hard to discover or operate.
- The archive offers filters or sorting without showing the active state, result period, or relationship between controls.
- Long-form openings omit ancestry, date, title, or a clear transition into the article body.
- Typography uses extreme scale, narrow measure, low contrast, or decorative family changes that weaken comprehension.
- Mixed article, report, case-study, press, and video formats look identical and provide no commitment cue.
- A lead story occupies most of the viewport while compact supporting stories lack enough metadata to be judged.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating editorial style as a serif font plus oversized imagery without defining content hierarchy or reading behavior.
- Using a masonry or irregular grid whose visual differences do not map to priority, format, or chronology.
- Overlaying essential headline and metadata on busy imagery without a reliable contrast treatment.
- Making the entire card clickable while also hiding the visible continuation cue or mixed-media destination.
- Adding every available taxonomy, author field, date, read time, tag, and format marker to every card.
- Rotating lead stories automatically and requiring readers to wait or navigate sequentially to inspect them.
- Using publication-like whitespace to remove useful search, filtering, archive, or contact utilities.
- Letting a branded editorial subsite use unrelated navigation, metadata, and interaction conventions.
- Using decorative pull quotes or metrics that interrupt the narrative but add no new evidence or structure.
- Replicating desktop lead-plus-sidebar layouts on narrow screens without defining semantic reading order.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Is there a clear lead story or idea, a bounded supporting set, and a distinct path into the larger collection?
- Do card scale, typography, image treatment, and placement map to real differences in priority, format, or reading path?
- Can each story be understood and compared through adjacent headline, deck or synopsis, category or format, date, author or publisher, and reading scope as needed?
- Do comparable items preserve one content grammar even when image, scale, or media type varies?
- Are search, topic, period, type, sort, and archive controls separate from story actions, with the active browse state visible?
- Are continuation links, card boundaries, mixed-media cues, and carousel controls perceivable and operable without relying on image interpretation, hover, or motion?
- Does typography create publication voice while preserving readable measure, sufficient contrast, localization tolerance, and narrow-screen fit?
- Do immersive article or report openings preserve ancestry, date, title, and a clear transition into the body?
- Does the narrow-screen order preserve the same lead, support, metadata, and archive relationships as the wide layout?
- Can readers choose between fast relevance scanning and deeper reading without losing their collection context?
