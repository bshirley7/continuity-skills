# Multimodal equivalence and sensory agency

## Generalized principles

- Define equivalence by access to essential information, context, interaction and outcome rather than identical presentation: identify what speech, meaningful sound, action, expression, visual change, on-screen text, timing and interactivity contribute; provide captions, description, descriptive transcripts, sign language or structural alternatives as the audience requires; and preserve language, sequence, semantics and actionable controls.
- Give people sensory and temporal agency before and throughout use: make alternatives and their status discoverable; expose captions and audio description with ordinary playback controls; support pause, stop, seek, replay, speed and self-paced text; allow presentation and language preferences without forcing diagnosis disclosure; and ensure settings, focus and auto-follow remain operable by keyboard and assistive technology.
- Treat quality as a multidimensional requirement, not the presence of a track: evaluate accuracy, synchronization, completeness, placement, readable duration, speaker identity, meaningful sounds, important visuals, language and dialect; identify generated, translated, live, reviewed and corrected states; enable repair and re-publication; and test comprehension and task completion with affected users.
- Design live and changing media for honest participation: disclose availability, language, latency, speaker and interpretation limits, outages and partial coverage; provide fallback and a corrected record when appropriate; avoid using meeting captions as a substitute for captions embedded in host-controlled media; and preserve equitable access when playback, network or processing conditions change.
- Protect privacy and dignity around access preferences and captured communication: do not require a person to reveal diagnosis to obtain alternatives; distinguish personal preferences from shared-device defaults; give notice and meaningful control over live transcription, recording, retention, access, correction and deletion; avoid treating one modality as the normal experience and alternatives as burdensome exceptions.
- Validate multimodal access across real combinations of disability, language, literacy, cognition, environment, bandwidth, device and consequence: include people who are Deaf, hard of hearing, blind, low vision, deafblind, neurodivergent or temporarily constrained; test both synchronized and independent alternatives; measure understanding, navigation, completion, fatigue, error recovery and preference persistence rather than counting accessibility labels.

## Method

- Identify the essential information, emotion, timing, navigation and action carried by each sensory channel.
- Map relevant permanent, temporary and situational access needs together with language, literacy, device and environment.
- Choose complementary alternatives and controls that preserve the outcome, then expose availability and limitations before commitment.
- Test synchronization, quality, correction, focus, preference scope, live degradation, privacy and post-event access.
- Evaluate comprehension and task completion through each supported modality combination and record unresolved inequivalence.

## Ethical safeguards

- Do not claim equivalence merely because captions or a transcript file exists.
- Do not require diagnosis, accommodation history or public disclosure to expose ordinary access controls.
- Do not publish generated or translated alternatives as human-verified when they are not.
- Do not let captions obscure essential visuals or make description compete with essential speech.
- Do not force audio, motion, flashing or autoplay before a person can express a preference.
- Do not capture, retain or share live speech more broadly than participants were told and allowed to control.
- Do not assume subtitles replace captions, transcripts replace synchronized alternatives, or one sign language serves every audience.
- Do not hide missing language, delay, outage, partial coverage or inaccessible interactive content.
- Do not evaluate access solely with automated conformance checks or nondisabled proxy users.

## Variation levers

- Increase human review and specialist production with consequence, technical vocabulary, multilingual content, rapid dialogue, multiple speakers and live use.
- Provide both synchronized and independent alternatives when users need contextual following, search, slower reading or deafblind access.
- Apply preferences at the narrowest useful scope on shared or sensitive devices and the broadest safe scope on personal devices.
- Use integrated description, alternate tracks, extended pauses or a separate equivalent version according to content density and production stage.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- Immediate automated access can be preferable to no access while creating errors that materially change meaning.
- Customized presentation improves access while increasing control complexity and inconsistent rendering.
- Persistent preferences reduce setup burden while revealing disability-related choices or becoming wrong in a new context.
- Synchronized following supports orientation while competing with self-paced reading and focus stability.
- Live transcription enables participation while expanding collection and retention of sensitive speech.

## Failure modes

- A media alternative repeats words but omits meaningful sound, visuals, interaction or timing.
- Controls for accessibility are less visible or less operable than volume and playback.
- Generated captions are presented without language, provenance, review or uncertainty state.
- Transcript auto-scroll cannot be paused and repeatedly steals focus.
- A live failure is hidden while participants believe an equivalent record exists.
- Preference scope is unclear and leaks to shared devices or fails to persist on personal ones.
- The team tests a caption indicator but not comprehension, navigation or task completion.
- One alternative is assumed to satisfy all sensory, language and cognitive needs.

## Anti-patterns

- File equals equivalence.
- Second-class controls.
- Automation without provenance.
- Captive transcript focus.
- Invisible live gap.
- Unscoped preference.
- Badge-only testing.
- One modality fits all.

## Acceptance and review questions

- What essential information, context, timing, interaction and outcome does each sensory channel carry, and where is each preserved?
- Can people discover and operate alternatives with ordinary controls before and throughout use without revealing a diagnosis?
- Are accuracy, synchronization, completeness, placement, readability, speaker, sound, visual, language and provenance quality explicitly assessed?
- Are live latency, partial coverage, failure, fallback and corrected post-event access honest and usable?
- Are preference scope and transcription consent, retention, access, correction and deletion safe on personal and shared devices?
- Have affected users demonstrated understanding, navigation and task completion across relevant modality, language, environment, bandwidth and device combinations?
