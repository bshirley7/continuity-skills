# Value first

## Generalized principles

- Lead with a recognizable user outcome or completed state before naming the product category, technology, or company ambition.
- Make the value concrete by naming the relevant input, task, change, or result closely enough that the intended audience can judge whether it applies.
- Follow the outcome immediately with the mechanism that produces it, using process detail, workflow structure, or a legible product state rather than assertion alone.
- Match evidence to the claim: show product or process evidence for capability, adoption evidence for confidence, and qualifications or safeguards for consequential promises.
- Keep the outcome, mechanism, evidence, and next action in one coherent decision sequence so users do not have to reconstruct why they should proceed.
- Use an action that continues the promised outcome, with a lower-commitment inspection path only when users may reasonably need more evidence before acting.
- Narrow broad or emotional value through an explicit audience, situation, task, or measurable boundary before asking for commitment.
- State material eligibility, effort, timing, price, or limitation near a high-consequence value claim so the promise remains credible at first evaluation.

## Variation levers

- Describe an input-to-output transformation when users already recognize the starting task, and lead with a completed state when the starting conditions vary.
- Use one outcome for a focused offer or decompose a broad outcome into a small set of user tasks when the service spans several jobs.
- Use a product state for software capability, process steps for services, adoption proof for confidence, and safeguards or qualifications for consequential decisions.
- Use one direct action when readiness is high, and pair it with an inspection action when the mechanism, risk, or fit requires evaluation.
- Place audience or situation context before the outcome when relevance is narrow, and immediately after it when the outcome is widely recognizable.
- Keep supporting detail compact in a hero and continue the same sequence in the next section when proof or qualification requires more space.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- A short outcome is easy to scan while often becoming vague or interchangeable across competitors.
- Specific task language improves relevance while excluding users who do not recognize themselves in the stated situation.
- Visible mechanism increases credibility while adding complexity before the primary action.
- Emotional outcomes create aspiration while requiring concrete task or boundary language to remain believable.
- A second inspection action supports cautious users while competing with the direct action for hierarchy.
- Qualifications protect trust while reducing the apparent simplicity or magnitude of the value claim.
- Product imagery demonstrates capability while lifestyle imagery may communicate identity or emotion more effectively.

## Failure modes

- The headline names a category, technology, or ambition without a recognizable user result.
- The outcome is so broad that it could describe most competing offers.
- Supporting text lists features without explaining how they produce the promised change.
- Customer marks, testimonials, or awards appear before users understand what the evidence is meant to support.
- Decorative or lifestyle imagery substitutes for a product, process, or service mechanism that users need to inspect.
- The next action is absent, generic, or disconnected from the outcome named above it.
- A consequential promise omits visible eligibility, effort, timing, cost, or limitation until a later step.
- Several outcomes, proof types, and actions compete without a clear primary decision sequence.

## Anti-patterns

- Leading with an unqualified superlative, category label, or company mission that leaves the user's change unspecified.
- Using an emotional metaphor whose concrete meaning appears only after substantial reading.
- Placing a wall of logos or a testimonial where the mechanism or user outcome should be.
- Showing a polished interface crop that does not demonstrate the claimed task or result.
- Using a generic learn-more action when the section promises a specific task the user can begin.
- Adding direct commitment and inspection actions with equal visual weight and no readiness distinction.
- Hiding material qualifications in later fine print to preserve a simpler first impression.

## Acceptance and review questions

- Can the intended audience identify the promised change before encountering product category or technology language?
- Does the value name an input, task, situation, or completed result specific enough to judge relevance?
- Can the user see how the product or service produces the outcome?
- Does each proof element support the nearby claim rather than supplying generic credibility?
- Do outcome, mechanism, evidence, and action form one understandable sequence?
- Does the primary action continue the promised task, and does any secondary action serve a distinct evidence need?
- Are broad emotional promises narrowed through audience, situation, task, or boundary language?
- Are material eligibility, effort, timing, price, and limitations visible where users first evaluate consequential value?
- Would the section remain meaningful if brand names, awards, customer marks, and decorative imagery were removed?
