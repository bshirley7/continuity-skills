# Motivation, progress, and temporal agency

## Generalized principles

- Anchor motivation to a user-chosen outcome: record what the person values, why the activity matters to them, what completion means, what alternatives exist, and how they can revise, pause, delegate, or stop; distinguish autonomous pursuit from compliance driven by guilt, pressure, reward loss, social judgment, or organizational metrics.
- Represent progress as evidence, not persuasion: define the true denominator, qualifying work, current state, uncertainty, dependencies, resets, credited and remaining effort, and completion authority; distinguish started, accepted, queued, processing, verified, and complete, and never use endowed, decorative, or illusionary advancement to create obligation.
- Make time legible and controllable: identify whose clock governs, start and update times, elapsed and expected duration, confidence or range, deadline basis, expiry effect, pause behavior, and recovery; let people extend, defer, save, stop, or choose an untimed alternative wherever timing is not intrinsic.
- Use milestones and subgoals to support planning, feedback, and recovery rather than pressure: make each milestone meaningful to the outcome, preserve the relationship to the whole, expose blocked and changed work, and avoid multiplying trivial steps, near-complete states, streaks, or resets solely to increase persistence.
- Design disengagement as a valid self-regulatory outcome: help people recognize changed value, unattainable conditions, excessive cost, conflict, harm, or uncertainty; preserve work and evidence, explain consequences, support pause or revised goals, and do not frame stopping as failure or make sunk effort a reason to continue.
- Bound prompts, rewards, and continuation mechanics by user control and cumulative exposure: state the initiating goal and sender, use the least disruptive channel, support schedules and quiet periods, provide natural stopping points, and prohibit endless feeds, autoplay, variable reward, escalating streak loss, or personalized pressure that weakens reflective choice.
- Evaluate durable value and agency beyond completion: measure accurate progress understanding, voluntary continuation and stopping, estimate calibration, interruption recovery, delayed regret, dependency, compulsive use, accessibility, and whether the completed outcome still serves the person; segment repeated exposure and harms rather than treating engagement as motivation.

## Method

- Define the user-chosen outcome, motivation, completion evidence, alternatives, cost, time horizon, dependencies, and valid pause or stop conditions.
- Model true lifecycle states, denominator, credited work, remaining effort, uncertainty, ownership, deadlines, expiry, and recovery.
- Inventory every prompt, reward, milestone, streak, progress cue, estimate, wait, interruption, renewal, reset, and exit across repeated exposure.
- Prototype start, ordinary progress, block, delay, regression, interruption, changed goal, pause, abandonment, resumption, and verified completion.
- Test comprehension of progress and time, voluntary continuation, refusal, stopping, recovery, estimate calibration, and delayed value.
- Monitor cumulative exposure, compulsive patterns, accessibility, regret, support demand, and subgroup harm alongside task outcomes.

## Ethical safeguards

- Do not manufacture progress, urgency, scarcity, completion proximity, social obligation, reward loss, or time pressure.
- Do not use guilt, shame, streak loss, sunk effort, variable reward, autoplay, or endless continuation to prevent reflective stopping.
- Do not treat engagement, retention, completion, or repeated return as evidence that the activity serves the person.
- Do not impose a timer, expiry, reset, or interruption when the underlying task does not require it.
- Do not personalize pressure using age, distress, disability, financial constraint, sleep, dependency, or inferred susceptibility.
- Do not make pause, stop, exit, quiet settings, or an untimed path harder to find or use than continuation.

## Variation levers

- Use explicit milestones for complex dependent work and lightweight state for short routine tasks.
- Show ranges and confidence for uncertain work; show exact time only when the source can support it.
- Use user-authored reminders and plans before system-authored prompts or behavioral pressure.
- Increase stopping points, reflection, and exposure controls with recurrence, vulnerability, personalization, or delayed cost.
- Preserve dense expert progress views while offering plain-language summaries and accessible alternatives.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- Visible progress supports planning while increasing pressure or attachment to sunk effort.
- Milestones make long work tractable while fragmenting the real outcome into performative completion.
- Estimates support coordination while creating false precision and expectation anchoring.
- Reminders support chosen plans while becoming interruption, surveillance, or controlled motivation.
- Rewards can acknowledge achievement while displacing intrinsic value or creating dependency.
- Easy disengagement preserves agency while reducing continuity for goals people genuinely value.

## Failure modes

- A progress bar advances by page count even though required work and verification remain unknown.
- An estimate is repeatedly reset instead of preserving the original promise and uncertainty.
- A streak or near-complete state makes stopping feel like losing previously earned value.
- The product treats queue acceptance or optimistic state as completion.
- Reminders multiply across channels without a user-authored plan or shared state.
- A changed or unattainable goal cannot be paused or retired without losing work or identity.
- Engagement rises while sleep, regret, task value, autonomy, or wellbeing worsens.

## Anti-patterns

- Progress by screen count.
- Always almost done.
- Reset the estimate.
- Streak as obligation.
- Reward without value.
- Queued means complete.
- Stopping means failure.
- Engagement means motivation.
- Wellbeing setting excuses endless design.

## Acceptance and review questions

- What user-chosen outcome and motivation does the activity serve, and can the person revise, pause, delegate, or stop it?
- Does progress reflect a true denominator, qualifying work, lifecycle state, uncertainty, dependencies, and verified completion?
- Are elapsed time, expected duration, confidence, deadline basis, expiry, pause, and recovery accurate and controllable?
- Do milestones support planning and recovery without artificial advancement, trivial fragmentation, near-complete pressure, or resets?
- Can a person stop or change an unattainable, harmful, costly, or no-longer-valued goal without losing evidence or being shamed?
- Are prompts, rewards, streaks, feeds, autoplay, and continuation bounded by user control, natural stopping points, and cumulative-exposure review?
- Does evaluation measure agency, comprehension, estimate calibration, stopping, recovery, delayed regret, dependency, accessibility, and durable value beyond engagement?
