# Perception, salience, and affordance

## Generalized principles

- Define the perceptual structure from task meaning before styling: establish reading order, regions, groups, alignment, containment, proximity, similarity, separation, and responsive transformation; make the same relationships available through semantic structure and do not assume visual adjacency alone survives reflow, zoom, localization, or assistive presentation.
- Use a bounded salience hierarchy tied to user consequence: rank what must be noticed, understood, acted on, monitored, or safely ignored; coordinate position, size, contrast, spacing, typography, color, depth, imagery, and motion without letting many simultaneous accents compete or letting decoration outrank state, evidence, warnings, or recovery.
- Make action, focus, selection, and state recognizable through redundant signifiers available before commitment: combine explicit language, conventional form, placement, boundary, contrast, iconography, and programmatic semantics; never rely on color, hover, aesthetic polish, familiarity, or one sensory cue as the only evidence that an element is interactive or important.
- Treat target size and spacing as both motor and perceptual requirements: give controls sufficient bounds and separation, preserve a clear association between label and target, prevent nearby actions from appearing as one group or causing accidental activation, and test touch, pointer, keyboard, zoom, tremor, limited precision, and crowded content.
- Treat aesthetics as a contributor to expectation, confidence, recognition, and attention rather than proof of usability or truth: evaluate perceived quality separately from observed task success, comprehension, error, accessibility, and recovery; test complete representative screens and content because attractive composition can support or compete with visual search.
- Evaluate selective attention under realistic sequence and repetition: test whether critical content remains discoverable amid dense information, repeated banners, familiar chrome, strong imagery, scrolling, interruption, fatigue, and prior expectations; preserve durable contextual status and alternate discovery paths instead of assuming visual prominence guarantees notice.
- Preserve hierarchy as presentation changes: maintain reading and focus order, primary action, grouping, comparison, status, and recovery across narrow and wide layouts, large text, high contrast, reduced transparency, absent imagery, color-vision differences, and right-to-left language; when visual order changes, verify that semantic and interaction order still describe the same task.

## Method

- Inventory the task, objects, decisions, states, warnings, evidence, actions, and recovery paths before assigning visual weight.
- Draw the intended reading, scanning, focus, and action sequence for representative novice, expert, low-vision, keyboard, touch, and interrupted use.
- Specify grouping and hierarchy through semantic regions, alignment, proximity, similarity, separation, labels, and redundant state cues.
- Prototype sparse, typical, dense, error, loading, stale, localized, zoomed, high-contrast, and narrow presentations.
- Test first notice, action recognition, visual search, task comprehension, selection accuracy, focus tracking, accidental activation, and recovery separately from aesthetic preference.
- Compare intended salience with observed attention and task behavior, recording overlooked critical content, decorative competition, false affordances, and subgroup differences.

## Ethical safeguards

- Do not use visual weight, isolation, contrast, animation, or placement to suppress a valid alternative or make refusal, correction, or exit less perceivable.
- Do not treat attention capture as evidence of comprehension, agreement, value, safety, or successful task completion.
- Do not let brand polish, prestige, aesthetic preference, or perceived ease substitute for observed usability, accessibility, or claim evidence.
- Do not encode critical meaning only through color, shape, position, motion, imagery, hover, or sound.
- Do not exploit fatigue, interruption, visual impairment, low literacy, unfamiliarity, or time pressure to steer consequential action.
- Do not average away people who miss a critical cue, misidentify an action, or cannot perceive the intended hierarchy.

## Variation levers

- Use stricter repetition and alignment for comparison and expert scanning; allow bounded asymmetry for narrative emphasis when return to order remains clear.
- Increase redundant labels, boundaries, and persistent status as novelty, consequence, density, or sensory variability rises.
- Use stronger salience for time-critical user harm and quieter contextual treatment for low-consequence informational state.
- Reduce simultaneous accents and decorative competition in dense, assistive, safety-critical, financial, health, and recovery contexts.
- Use familiar platform signifiers for frequent actions and reserve custom forms for identity moments whose behavior remains explicit.

## Tensions and tradeoffs

- Stronger hierarchy accelerates scanning while narrowing attention to information the designer selected.
- Visual consistency supports recognition while making exceptional state easier to overlook.
- Aesthetic restraint reduces competition while weakening brand expression or emotional orientation.
- Redundant signifiers improve access while increasing visual density.
- Large targets and spacing improve perception and motor access while reducing visible context.
- Novel visual treatment creates distinctiveness while increasing interpretation and testing burden.

## Failure modes

- Every region uses accent color, cards, large type, imagery, or motion and no stable priority remains.
- Critical information is technically present but repeatedly filtered out because it resembles advertising, chrome, or low-priority status.
- A control is recognizable only after hover, selection, failure, or prior product knowledge.
- Responsive reordering changes the apparent relationship, reading order, or action consequence.
- A polished screen receives high preference scores while task errors, missed warnings, or inaccessible state remain unmeasured.
- Design review approves isolated components without testing the cumulative hierarchy of a realistic page or workflow.

## Anti-patterns

- Make it pop.
- Everything is primary.
- Color means enough.
- Hover reveals action.
- Beautiful therefore usable.
- Present therefore noticed.
- Desktop order with mobile wrapping.
- Preference score as task evidence.

## Acceptance and review questions

- What task meaning determines the intended reading, grouping, salience, focus, and action order?
- Can every material relationship and state survive reflow, zoom, localization, high contrast, absent imagery, and assistive presentation?
- Are actions, focus, selection, and state recognizable before interaction through more than one perceivable and semantic cue?
- Do target size and spacing support accurate touch, pointer, keyboard, zoomed, and limited-precision use?
- Are aesthetic preference, perceived ease, observed task success, comprehension, accessibility, error, and recovery measured separately?
- Which critical elements were overlooked under realistic density, repetition, scrolling, interruption, fatigue, and expectation?
- Does any visual emphasis suppress an alternative, material fact, refusal, correction, exit, or recovery path?
